*What am I actually studying?*

After a few weeks of studying Local Development, I thought it might be a good idea to start documenting what I am learning, the concepts that make me stop and think, and perhaps some questions that I do not yet have answers to.

I used to think about writing academic summaries after each course or module, with references and all the relevant theories. However, I would rather keep this space a little more personal. I want to share not only what I learn but also how I understand it, question it, and sometimes struggle with it.

So, let's start with a simple question.

**"What are you studying in Italy?"**

I am studying for a Master's degree in Local Development at the University of Padua. Before this, I studied International Relations in Vietnam for four years. My previous studies introduced me to international politics, diplomacy, global governance, international cooperation, and the relationships between countries.

So, why Local Development?

I have always been interested in development, particularly the questions surrounding inequality, education, migration, and the different opportunities available to people depending on where they are born and where they live. However, I gradually realised that looking at development only through international relations and national policies was not enough. I wanted to understand what happens at the local level: how people experience development in their everyday lives, how communities respond to changes, and how decisions made far away can shape the futures of people living in a particular place.

Local Development is an interdisciplinary field that brings together geography, economics, sociology, politics, planning, and other areas of knowledge. It looks at how places develop, how different actors interact, and how local resources, institutions, relationships, and power structures shape development processes.

At least, this is how I would explain it for now.

As I am writing this reflection, I ask myself: What are the ideas that have surprised me the most during these first weeks of studying? Three concepts immediately come to mind.

## *Development is not simply economic growth*

One of the first things we encounter when studying development is the question of what development actually means.

For a long time, development has been closely associated with economic growth, industrialisation, modernisation, and increasing living standards. A country with a higher GDP, better infrastructure, and a growing economy is often considered more developed.

In one of our discussions, we encountered modernisation theory and Walt Rostow's Stages of Economic Growth. Rostow proposed that societies move through different stages of economic development, eventually reaching an age of high mass consumption. This understanding presents development as a relatively linear process, where countries can follow a particular trajectory towards modernisation.

At first glance, this seems quite straightforward. If economic growth improves people's living conditions, why shouldn't we consider it development?

But then I started thinking about the assumptions behind this idea.

Does every society need to follow the same path to become developed? Who decides what a developed society should look like? And what happens to the people who do not benefit from economic growth?

A country can experience rapid economic growth while inequalities continue to increase. A new infrastructure project might improve connectivity and attract investment, but it could also displace local communities. An area might become more economically productive while losing some of its cultural and environmental resources.

This made me realise that development is not just about measuring how much a country or a place grows. It is also about understanding who benefits, who bears the costs, and whose definition of progress is being followed.

I find this particularly interesting because it challenges the way development is often presented as something universally positive. Development is also a political process, involving different interests, priorities, and sometimes conflicting visions of the future.

Perhaps the question should not only be how to develop, but also what kind of development we want and for whom.

## *The local is not isolated from the global*

Coming from an International Relations background, I used to look at many issues from the perspective of international institutions, national governments, and global cooperation.

Studying Local Development has made me reconsider the relationship between different geographical scales.

What does local actually mean? Is it simply a village, a city, a region, or a smaller geographical unit within a country?

I am beginning to understand that the local is much more than a geographical boundary. A place is shaped by its history, culture, institutions, economic activities, social relationships, and the people who live there.

More importantly, the local does not exist independently from the national or global context.

For example, decisions made by international organisations or national governments can have direct consequences for the livelihoods of people in rural communities. Global trade policies can affect local farmers. Climate change can disrupt local economies and force people to migrate. Meanwhile, local communities can also influence broader political and economic processes through their collective actions.

This makes me think about the concept of scale and the relationships between the local, national, regional, and global levels.

I used to imagine these levels as separate layers, but I am learning that they are deeply interconnected. Something that happens at the global level can be experienced very differently across local contexts.

This is also why I find Local Development particularly interesting. It allows me to connect the bigger political and economic structures that I studied before with the everyday experiences of people and communities.

I am curious about how these different scales interact, how power moves between them, and how local actors negotiate, adapt to, or resist decisions made at other levels.

## *Development is also about power*

This is probably the idea that has made me think the most so far.

When we talk about development, we often encounter words such as participation, empowerment, inclusion, sustainability, and community engagement. These concepts sound positive, and they are frequently used in development policies and projects.

But I have started asking myself: What happens behind these words?

Who decides what a community needs? Who defines the problems that a development project should address? Who controls the resources? Whose knowledge is considered important? And who actually has the power to make decisions?

Imagine a development project introduced to a rural community. It promises better infrastructure, employment opportunities, and improved living conditions. The project might be designed by government authorities, international organisations, or development agencies, with the intention of improving people's lives.

But what if the local community was not meaningfully involved in deciding what should happen? What if the project affected their livelihoods, land, or cultural practices? What if their understanding of development was different from the vision proposed by the people implementing the project?

Would we still consider it successful?

I think these questions are important because development is never entirely neutral. Different actors have different interests, resources, and levels of influence. Some have the power to define problems and propose solutions, while others are expected to adapt to decisions that have already been made.

This also makes me think about participation. Inviting people to a meeting or asking them to complete a survey does not necessarily mean that they have the power to influence a decision.

There is a difference between being informed, being consulted, participating in a process, and actually having decision-making power.

For me, this is where the study of Local Development becomes particularly connected to the study of power. It is not enough to understand whether a development project achieves its objectives. We also need to understand who sets those objectives, whose interests they represent, and who gets to define what success means.

I would like to explore this relationship further, especially in the context of communities that have historically had less influence over decisions affecting their lives.

## *Is there only one way to develop?*

Another question that has stayed with me is whether there is a universal model of development that every society should follow.

Much of what we traditionally understand as development has been influenced by Western experiences of industrialisation, economic growth, and modernisation. These experiences have often become reference points for evaluating other societies.

But should every community aspire to become the same kind of society?

This question brings me to ideas such as post-development and decolonial approaches, which challenge some of the assumptions underlying conventional development thinking.

What interests me is the question of who has historically had the authority to define development. Why are certain forms of knowledge considered more legitimate than others? Why are some ways of living described as traditional or underdeveloped, while others are presented as modern and advanced?

I find these questions particularly relevant when thinking about rural communities and indigenous peoples, whose relationships with land, nature, and their surroundings might not fit into conventional economic measurements.

For example, land is not necessarily just an economic resource. For some communities, it is connected to identity, memory, cultural practices, belonging, and relationships between generations.

If development is measured primarily through economic productivity, how do we account for these other dimensions of people's lives?

I do not have a clear answer yet. However, I find it important to question the idea that there is only one definition of progress, or one path that all societies should follow.

Perhaps development should also involve the ability of communities to define their own priorities and imagine different futures.

## *So, where do I go from here?*

These first weeks have been an introduction to a field that is much broader than I initially expected.

I am beginning to see that studying Local Development means engaging with many different disciplines and perspectives. Geography helps me understand places and spatial relationships. Sociology introduces me to social structures and inequalities. Political science helps me examine institutions and power. Economics offers different ways of understanding resources, production, and livelihoods.

And somehow, all these perspectives come together when we try to understand how a particular place develops.

I still have many questions, and I am not sure which direction I will eventually take.

I find myself returning to several topics that have interested me for a long time: migration, climate change, displacement, education, and inequality. I am particularly curious about how environmental and economic changes affect people's relationships with the places they live, and how these changes shape the opportunities available to young people and marginalised communities.

Coming from Vietnam, I also find myself thinking about rural communities, particularly those that face geographical and socioeconomic inequalities. However, I want to keep my perspective open and learn from different contexts before deciding where to focus.

Perhaps this is what I appreciate most about being at the beginning of a new academic journey. I do not need to have everything figured out yet.

For now, I want to learn how to ask better questions, understand different perspectives, and become more critical of the concepts that I might previously have taken for granted.

I started this Master's programme thinking I would learn how to understand and support local development. A few weeks in, I am beginning to realise that I first need to understand what development means, who gets to define it, and what it means for the people who experience it.

My learning journey has just begun, and I hope to share more reflections along the way.

Thank you for reading. Until the next reflection!
